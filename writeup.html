DSC 106 PROJECT 3
Visualization Write-up
GFDL-ESM4 · 1950–2014

Project Write-up

Exploring geographic and seasonal patterns in global warming, 1950–2014.

1. Motivation & Research Question

Our visualization was motivated by the question of how warming rates differ across seasons, geographic regions, and time periods. While global temperature trends provide an overall picture of climate change, they can obscure differences between seasons and locations. We wanted to create an exploratory visualization that allows users to investigate these differences and understand how they developed over time.

2. Visualization Design

We designed the visualization around two coordinated views: a geographic map and a temporal chart. The map shows where seasonal warming rates differ, while the temporal chart shows the underlying temperature anomalies and fitted trends over time. We chose to keep both views visible because each provides context for interpreting the other. This allows users to notice geographic patterns without losing sight of the temporal trends underlying them.

3. Data Transformations & Visual Encodings

We used geographic position to preserve spatial relationships and aggregated the data into 5° × 5° latitude-longitude tiles. This balances geographic detail with readability and reduces visual clutter, although it sacrifices some finer spatial variation. The visualization compares seasonal warming rates using differences between fitted temperature trends rather than simply comparing absolute temperature values.

We assigned each season a consistent categorical color across the selectors, map, and temporal chart. While using categorical colors alongside a quantitative difference could introduce some ambiguity, consistent colors reduce the mental effort of identifying and comparing seasons across views.

Centering the map's color scale at zero allows users to distinguish which season has the higher warming rate, while color intensity communicates the magnitude of the difference. A neutral midpoint and labeled legend clarify that the map represents differences in warming rates rather than absolute temperatures.

4. Interactions & Exploration

Our main interactions include a moving 30-year analysis window, geographic selection through clicking or brushing, and linked updates between the map and temporal chart. These interactions allow users to explore how seasonal warming-rate differences vary across locations and periods while examining the underlying trends. We also included a full-period option to provide a broader historical reference.

The intended exploration sequence is to change the time period, select a geographic area, and compare the corresponding seasonal trends. Linking these interactions helps users move between discovering spatial patterns and examining their temporal context.

5. Design Alternatives & Trade-offs

We considered several alternatives, including quantile-based filtering, a ranked horizontal bar chart, and a scatterplot. For example, a ranked bar chart could highlight regions with the largest differences but would lose the geographic relationships between them.

Other approaches introduced additional complexity without making the patterns substantially easier to interpret. We ultimately prioritized coordinated geographic and temporal views with a simple interaction sequence, allowing users to explore the data without navigating multiple disconnected visualizations.

6. Team Contributions & Development

Our team worked collaboratively to develop and refine the initial design document, discussing the research question, visual encodings, and intended interaction flow. Once we had a clear design, we used a coding agent to generate an initial implementation quickly, emphasizing separation between data processing, visualization, and interaction logic.

Aedan took primary responsibility for implementing the application and iteratively refining the visualizations, interactions, and layout. The other team members contributed to design decisions, reviewed the implementation, and provided feedback on usability and whether the intended exploratory workflow was intuitive. Team members also had opportunities to make further improvements based on these reviews and their own observations.

7. Iteration & Challenges

Our development process was iterative, involving repeated adjustments to the layout, visual encodings, and interactions based on testing and feedback. We estimate that the project required approximately 15–20 people-hours in total.

The most time-consuming aspect was refining the coordinated views and their interactions, particularly ensuring that users could move naturally between geographic and temporal comparisons. Establishing a clear and intuitive user experience required more iteration than the initial implementation itself.
